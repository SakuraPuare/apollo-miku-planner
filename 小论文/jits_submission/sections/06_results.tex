\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{RQ1: Main comparison}
\label{subsec:results_main}

The fixed-section test found exact agreement between the $O(k\log k)$ scan and exhaustive enumeration for all 4,000 generated instances. The maximum absolute center-band-width difference was below the $10^{-12}$ numerical test tolerance. This result establishes the theorem's fixed-section claim only; it does not imply global optimality of the complete trajectory planner.

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the 3,500 paired scenarios. MIKU increased collision-free goal reaching from 62.69\% for B0 to 77.46\%, a paired difference of 14.77 percentage points (95\% CI 13.54--15.97; exact McNemar $p=1.94\times10^{-133}$). Collision decreased from 2.17\% to 1.14\% (difference $-1.03$ percentage points, 95\% CI $-1.37$ to $-0.71$; $p=2.91\times10^{-11}$). Mean progress increased by 8.93 percentage points, jerk RMS decreased by 0.120 m/s$^3$, and penalized travel time decreased by 0.81 s. The aggregate mean-clearance difference was $-0.010$ m with a 95\% CI of $[-0.024,0.004]$, so the success and collision gains cannot be interpreted as a uniform clearance increase.

The family-level effects in Figure~\ref{fig:evidence_dashboard} show where the overall difference originates. The largest success gain occurs in the narrow multi-obstacle family (65.4 percentage points), followed by delayed crossing (26.8 percentage points). Crossing-pedestrian and vehicle cut-in families show no aggregate success difference in this protocol, which is consistent with cases in which the baseline already has a feasible temporal response. Prediction noise yields a 3.2-point success increase and a 7.0-point collision reduction, but progress decreases by 1.12 points and jerk RMS increases by 0.041 m/s$^3$.

The finite-grid reference provides a computational trade-off rather than a winner-takes-all comparison. In 70 cases, B3 reached 80.0\% of goals and MIKU 77.1\% (paired difference $-2.86$ percentage points, 95\% CI $[-11.43,5.71]$, $p=.754$). Median runtime was 1,835.43 ms for B3 and 10.44 ms for MIKU. Thus MIKU approaches the finite reference's success rate at a much lower measured cost in this small, coarse grid experiment, while the two solvers optimize different representations.

\subsection{RQ2: Mechanisms and ablations}
\label{subsec:results_ablation}

The subtractive ablations isolate mechanisms on the same 3,500 scenarios. Removing Top-$K$ spatial homotopy reduced success by 10.17 percentage points and removing the threat-aware margin reduced it by 10.74 points relative to full MIKU. Removing the temporal homotopy graph reduced delayed-crossing success from 89.6\% to 66.8\%, a 22.8-point family-specific loss. Removing the robust occupancy tube increased prediction-noise collision from 3.6\% to 11.0\%. Arrival projection and longitudinal grouping produce smaller aggregate changes, indicating that their value is concentrated in particular configurations rather than uniform across all families.

The mechanism cases provide a geometric explanation for these effects. In the narrow-passage case, the obstacle-wise policy raises and lowers opposing path bounds until the center interval becomes empty, whereas the group-level partition retains one continuous band. In the delayed-crossing family, the temporal graph preserves the before- and after-obstacle alternatives and enforces their causal order. These examples locate the failure mechanism; they are not substitutes for the family-level estimates.

\subsection{RQ3: Robustness, side effects, and latency tails}
\label{subsec:results_robustness}

The prediction-noise subgroup demonstrates both a benefit and a cost. MIKU reduces collisions by 7.0 percentage points relative to B0, but its mean progress is 59.73\% versus 60.85\% and its jerk RMS is 2.274 versus 2.234 m/s$^3$. This is consistent with uncertainty-expanded occupancy being conservative in cases where the error bound is large relative to the available passage.

The complete-call runtime median and P95 are similar to or lower than B0, but the P99 tail is not. MIKU's P99 is 221.80 ms, compared with 138.88 ms for B0; the empirical latency curve in Figure~\ref{fig:evidence_dashboard} shows the divergence in the upper tail. B2 is slower across all reported quantiles because it always performs three coordination iterations. These results support a typical-cycle efficiency statement, not a hard real-time guarantee.

The independent rolling-replanning protocol gives a second view of accumulated behavior. Across 700 cases, success increases from 62.29\% to 72.29\% (paired difference 10.0 percentage points, 95\% CI 7.57--12.57), while collision remains 1.29\% for both methods. Mean progress increases from 82.55\% to 90.25\%, but episode-runtime P95 increases from 827.20 to 1,361.51 ms. Because this protocol executes and replans rather than evaluating one open-loop call, it is reported as robustness evidence and not merged with Table~\ref{tab:main_results}.

\subsection{RQ4: PVD software-interface evidence}
\label{subsec:results_apollo}

The Apollo 11.0 implementation passes the generated path and ST constraints through the existing path-boundary and ST-boundary interfaces while retaining the downstream QP objectives and kinematic equalities. Three representative Dreamview cases show dynamic overtaking, narrow-passage traversal, and single-lane diversion. These cases demonstrate that the abstract constraint compilation has a compatible software insertion point and produces the intended qualitative behavior. They do not establish physical-road safety, native Apollo latency, hardware-in-the-loop performance, or complete end-to-end correctness.
