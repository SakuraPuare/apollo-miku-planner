# Paper 32 audit: *Robotaxis in China: Firsthand perspectives of traffic law enforcement officers from Chinese cities with operational fleets*

## Positioning, type, and audience

- **Domain/type:** empirical intelligent-transportation and transport-policy study of robotaxi acceptance, safety perception, and enforcement readiness. It is a cross-sectional online survey with descriptive analysis, contingency-table tests, and two ordered-logit models.
- **Bibliographic/form signal:** *Journal of Intelligent Transportation Systems* 30(3), 649–672; DOI `10.1080/15472450.2025.2497503`; received 28 Jan 2025, revised 11 Apr, accepted 21 Apr, online 8 May 2025. The title uses a strong journal-compatible formula: **technology + geography: distinctive evidence source/context**.
- **Primary audiences:** ITS and autonomous-mobility researchers; Chinese traffic-management and law-enforcement agencies; transport policymakers and urban planners; robotaxi operators/developers. Secondary audiences include safety/trust/technology-adoption scholars and first-responder training designers.

## Architecture and rhetorical moves

1. **Abstract:** one unstructured paragraph moving from deployment opportunity/challenge, to a neglected stakeholder, sample/method, headline findings, predictors, and policy value. It names the >4,000 response scale, Fisher tests/models, positive views, unresolved safety/compliance/cybersecurity concerns, and intended use.
2. **Introduction:** opens with a concrete service (Luobo Kuaipao/Apollo Go) and deployment statistics (Wuhan: 400 vehicles, planned 1,000; >6 million rides and >100 million km by July 2024), then balances promised benefits against enforcement problems. It narrows the gap from well-studied users to traffic police, states **six numbered research questions**, previews methods, and claims contribution through firsthand daily interaction.
3. **Literature review:** defines overlapping labels (robotaxi, driverless taxi, SAV), synthesizes acceptance drivers (safety, trust, reliability, demographics, experience, convenience), then repeats and sharpens the police-perspective gap.
4. **Online survey:** design → collection → ethics/selection caveat → cleaning → analysis/model equations. Results progress from demographics, exposure, enforcement experience, attitudes, concerns, to models. Discussion uses a repeated pattern: finding → possible mechanism → prior literature → intervention. Limitations precede a conclusion that recaps evidence and ends in policy actions.

## Survey, sampling, and ethics

- **Instrument:** five modules, documented with example questions in **Table 1**: automation knowledge/experience; enforcement experience; perceptions/attitudes; concerns/suggestions; demographics/job duty. Questions were asked in Chinese and translated for the paper (Table 1 note).
- **Recruitment:** Wenjuanxing smartphone survey distributed through traffic-police WeChat groups in Chinese cities with operational robotaxis by July 2024. This is explicitly called a **convenience sample**. More than 4,000 responded; **3,498** remained.
- **Cleaning:** excluded completions under three minutes, missing key attitude answers, and respondents who denied robotaxi operation in cities where it was known to exist.
- **Ethics:** prior informed consent; notice of analysis/publication; no identifiers; anonymity/confidentiality; voluntary participation, skipping and withdrawal permitted; no incentives or penalties.
- **Missing reproducibility detail:** no survey field dates, city-by-city counts, invitation denominator/response rate, recruitment quotas, full questionnaire, pilot/scale validation, ethics-board/IRB name or approval number, data/code availability statement, or cleaning-flow counts by exclusion reason. The rule discarding a respondent’s city answer based on authors’ external knowledge needs prespecification and sensitivity analysis.

## Data presentation and statistical reporting

- **Seven tables.** **Table 1** exposes instrument logic; **Table 2** profiles demographics/jobs; **Table 3** reports familiarity/news/driving/riding experience; **Table 4** reports incident experience, procedure knowledge, liability attribution, and unsuitable road contexts; **Table 5** gives all main perception/attitude distributions; **Tables 6–7** combine ordered-logit coefficients, p-values, five outcome-specific marginal effects, thresholds, and fit statistics.
- **Twelve figures, all empirical rather than conceptual.** **Figures 1–3** visualize familiarity cross-tabs with news, ADAS driving, and robotaxi riding. **Figures 4–6** compare driving/safety distributions and summarize command trust/overall attitude. **Figures 7–10** relate overall attitude to robotaxi behavior, safety, efficiency, and command trust. **Figures 11–12** rank multi-response concerns and recommended preparations. Tables preserve exact values; figures emphasize patterns, with prose interpreting both.
- **Descriptive style:** frequencies plus one-decimal percentages. Examples: 10.1% had ridden in a robotaxi (**Table 3**); only 26.7% knew incident procedures and 58.5% assigned primary responsibility to the operator (**Table 4**); support was 35.5%, neutral 39.8%, opposition 24.7%, while only 9.5% trusted vehicles to follow officer commands (**Table 5**). **Figure 11** highlights cybersecurity (74.4%), noncompliance/complex scenarios (63.9%), failures (56.7%), and privacy (53.7%); **Figure 12** leads with laws/regulations (83.7%), collaboration (69.1%), equipment (63.4%), and training (57.6%).
- **Testing/modeling:** sparse larger-than-2×2 tables motivated simulated-p-value Fisher exact tests after checking expected counts; ordered-logit equations are numbered **(1)–(2)**. Five-level dependent variables are safety (1 totally unsafe–5 totally safe) and overall attitude (1 strongly oppose–5 strongly support). Marginal effects translate coefficients into probability-point changes. **Table 6:** N=3,498, pseudo-R²=.282, AIC=6913.713; “very good” robotaxi behavior has β=5.309, p<.001 and +65.3 points across the two safe outcomes. **Table 7:** N=3,498, pseudo-R²=.255, AIC=7235.952; “very safe” has β=4.087, p<.001 and +63.5 points across support outcomes.

## Discussion, implications, and limitations

The discussion turns exposure/familiarity, ride experience, driving quality, safety, efficiency, command compliance, and procedural knowledge into proposals for demonstrations/ride-alongs, transparent safety communication, incident-response training, regulation, equipment upgrades, and operator–police collaboration. It also targets older officers and frontline roles. The limitations section explicitly covers self-report/social-desirability/recall bias, smartphone/WeChat selection, unknown population and non-generalizability, single-stakeholder scope, cross-sectional model sensitivity, and unobserved heterogeneity; it proposes diverse/longitudinal/multistakeholder work and advanced models.

## Front/back matter and house-style cues

Front matter includes affiliations, a single-paragraph abstract, article history, and five keywords. Citations are author–date, often clustered after synthesis claims. Tables have captions above; figure captions below; equations are numbered. Back matter contains Acknowledgments, Disclosure, Funding, and references. ## Reusable techniques and cautions for a writing skill

**Reuse:** anchor novelty in an operational setting; define stakeholder-specific research questions before methods; make instrument modules map visibly to RQs; pair complete distribution tables with selective visual comparisons; report base categories, coefficients, p-values, marginal effects, N, likelihoods, pseudo-R², and AIC; translate each model result into operational meaning; separate concerns from recommended preparations; disclose translation, recruitment, cleaning, ethics, funding, conflicts, and AI language assistance.

**Do not imitate uncritically:** distinguish perception from measured vehicle safety and association from causation. The claim that perceptions “indirectly support” high safety standards is not established by self-report. “Generally favorable” overstates **Table 5**, where neutrality is the largest category. Clarify whether reported χ² statistics for **Figures 7–10** come from chi-square tests or how they relate to the stated Fisher procedure; report simulated-test settings. Add confidence intervals/SEs, odds ratios where useful, missing-data accounting, multicollinearity checks, proportional-odds diagnostics, robustness/sensitivity analyses, and rationale for p-value-based variable removal at 0.10. Resolve internal slips: the conclusion names a “law enforcement perception model” despite only two specified models; strong support is 5.5% in Table 5 but 5.1% in prose; procedure “unsure” is 30.5% in Table 4 but 31.5% in prose. Verify captions and extracted layout against the source PDF before treating MinerU artifacts as journal rules.
