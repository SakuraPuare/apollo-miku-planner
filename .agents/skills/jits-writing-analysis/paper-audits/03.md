# Paper 03 audit: vehicle classification from pavement vibration

## Identity, domain, article type, and audience

- **Exact title:** “Vehicle classification using accelerometer signals and machine-learning techniques.” Amar Deep Pandey et al.; *Journal of Intelligent Transportation Systems* 30(4), 697–725 (2026); DOI `10.1080/15472450.2025.2497507`; received 25 October 2024, revised 11 April 2025, accepted 21 April 2025, and published online 7 May 2025.
- **Type/domain:** original empirical engineering research at the intersection of intelligent transportation systems, pavement-vibration sensing, signal processing, and supervised multiclass machine learning. It proposes and evaluates a single-sensor classification pipeline, rather than presenting a review or theory paper.
- **Likely primary audience:** transportation/civil engineers and ITS researchers. Secondary audiences are signal-processing and applied-ML researchers, road/traffic authorities, and operators of toll plazas, parking facilities, logistics hubs, and restricted-heavy-vehicle zones (Abstract; Section 1; Section 5).

## Overall structure and rhetorical progression

The publisher-facing front matter uses title, author/affiliation block, one-paragraph unstructured abstract, article history, six semicolon-separated keywords, and a graphical abstract. The body follows a conventional empirical sequence: **1 Introduction and literature review → 2 Methodology → 3 Results → 4 Discussion → 5 Conclusions and future scope**, followed by disclosure, funding, ORCID, data availability, references, and an acronym list.

The argument moves from societal/operational value, through shortcomings of intrusive and non-intrusive sensing, to a sharply stated sensor-complexity gap. Tables 1–2 turn the literature review into a sensor/accuracy/class comparison. The introduction then names the proposed mechanism and closes with three numbered objectives plus a manuscript roadmap (Section 1). Methods progressively narrow from site and hardware to signal cleaning, feature construction, imbalance treatment, model stacking, statistical analysis, reproducibility parameters, and pseudocode (Sections 2.1–2.8). Results first survey all experiments, then spotlight the winning configuration; Discussion explains components and cost/performance trade-offs; the conclusion restates the pipeline, headline result, practical value, limitations, and future work.

## Title, abstract, and introduction techniques

- The title is descriptive and searchable: **task + data source + method family**, with no inflated novelty adjective or result value.
- The Abstract executes seven compact moves: importance/use cases; limitations of alternatives; proposed single tri-axial sensor; ADASYN; Random Forest/XGBoost stack; headline `99.78%` accuracy over eight classes and 50 iterations; deployment relevance. This is a useful problem–method–result–implication template.
- Section 1 opens broadly with safety, congestion, emissions, enforcement, and planning, then organizes prior work by **intrusive versus non-intrusive** technologies. It quantifies the gap: a seven-accelerometer study reached about `95%`, whereas three accelerometers plus camera data reportedly reached `83%`. It converts that contrast into three objectives: low cost/environmental robustness, simpler installation, and robustness under imbalance.

## Methods, data, and validation

- Four Gorakhpur-area sites were selected after road-condition inspection. Over `20 d`, two people manually triggered and annotated `9,476` isolated single-vehicle pass-bys on dry, bituminous pavement (Sections 2.1.1–2.1.2; Figure 2; Table 7). Eight classes are explicitly counted: truck `4,391` (`46.3%`), two-wheeler `1,908`, car/SUV `1,763`, LCV `554`, e-rickshaw `301`, bus `289`, three-wheeler `159`, tractor `111` (`1.2%`).
- The single tri-axial 01 dB ACOEM sensor samples each axis at `12.8 kHz`, covers `6 kHz`, has `100 mV/g` sensitivity and an `80 g` full scale (Section 2.1.2; Table 7). A second-order Butterworth `1.2–500 Hz` band-pass removes DC/electronic and high-frequency background components; a `700 ms` rectangular cut removes filter transients (Section 2.2; Equations 1–4). Each event is reduced to the maximum-energy `1 s`/`12,800`-sample window with stated `0.1%` overlap and `12,787`-sample step (Section 2.3.1; Equations 5–6; Figure 7).
- Peak, RMS, and SUM (squared-amplitude energy) are extracted per axis, then tested as seven axis combinations for each feature family. These `21` variable sets cross six balancing/model regimes to create `126` configurations (Table 6). Quadratic interaction-only polynomial features are also reported (Table 7).
- ADASYN expands the classes to `35,091` observations total, with per-class counts `4,200–4,525`, reducing the stated majority/minority ratio from `39.56` to `1.08` (Table 5; Equation 7). The winning WBS stack uses Random Forest and XGBoost base learners with logistic-regression meta-learner; Table 7 specifies `max_iter=10,000`, `liblinear`, five-fold CV, `random_state=42`, and a nominal `60/20/20` train/validation/test split. Algorithm 1 makes the computational sequence inspectable and lists weighted precision/recall and multiclass one-vs-rest AUC.

This is a strong “one visual, one evidentiary job” pattern.

## Quantitative and statistical reporting

Set 85 (XYZ SUM + ADASYN + stacking) reports test loss `0.0337%`, accuracy/precision/recall/F1 `99.78%`, and AUC `1.00` (Section 3.1.1); training and validation accuracy are `99.76%` and `99.77%` (Table 10). Across 50 random repartitions, mean test accuracy is `99.774%`, SD `0.047%`, 95% CI `[99.761%, 99.787%]`, with one-sample `t=3.606`, `p=0.0007` (Section 3.2; Figure 12). Runtime spans `5.42–233.75 s`; Set 85 takes `229.89 s`, versus `40.82 s` and `74.75%` test accuracy for Set 2 (Section 4.4). The conclusion also offers a hardware trade-off: two-axis sensing at `96.86%` versus the three-axis best at `99.78%`.

## Discussion, limitations, conclusion, and disclosures

Discussion is mechanism-led: it attributes gains separately to balancing, stacking, and comprehensive XYZ energy, then compares fewer-axis alternatives (e.g., Set 91 XZ SUM: validation `97.33%`, test `97.09%`) and runtime. Limitations are explicit but late: isolated pass-bys only, dry bituminous roads, no non-motorized classes, pavement/environment sensitivity and recalibration; wet/concrete pavement, simultaneous vehicles, adaptive models, and weight tagging are future work (Sections 4.2–4.4; Section 5).

The authors declare no financial/personal conflict; funding is India IMPRINT grant `4583` (“Sustainable Habitat”); one ORCID is supplied; data are only “available upon request.” The custom mount is patent pending (Section 2.1.2; Table 7), but no code repository, author-contribution statement, or dedicated ethics statement appears.

## Reusable techniques and cautions for a JITS writing skill

**Reuse:** build an application-led gap; quantify comparisons; state numbered objectives; signpost sections; provide a complete workflow figure, notation table, design matrix, system-parameter table, equations, and algorithm; report class counts before/after balancing; separate Results from interpretation; combine point estimates, uncertainty, diagnostic plots, confusion matrices, ablations, and runtime; end with bounded limitations and deployment implications; include the complete declarations block.

**Do not copy uncritically:** Algorithm 1 scales and applies ADASYN **before** splitting (lines 521–531), creating likely train/test leakage; preprocessing and resampling should be fit inside training folds only. The same reported test results help select Set 85 from 126 configurations, so a nested or untouched final test is needed to avoid selection optimism. The `80/20` description in Section 2.5.3 conflicts with Table 7’s `60/20/20`, while Section 2.2 calls the transient cut both `700 microseconds` and `700 ms`. The t-test benchmark `mu_0` is never numerically identified, and significance does not establish “high for 95% of the time.” Fifty event-level re-splits of the same sites are not external, site-held-out, temporal, wet-road, or mixed-traffic validation. Hyperparameters and seeds are incomplete; data are not open. AUC is inconsistently written as `1.00`, `0.99`, and “`0.99%`.” The prose also repeats the winning result heavily, contains numbering/encoding defects, and sometimes converts association into component-wise causal claims without paired statistical tests. Treat these as paper-specific flaws, not journal conventions; one article alone cannot establish universal JITS rules.
